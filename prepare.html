How to prepare

How long to prepare, practice versus drilling, and a loop that works for multiple-choice sections.

Think in months and years

Strong candidates build reading, maths and reasoning steadily through upper primary, get familiar with the format the year before, and sharpen exam technique in the final months. Cramming tends to raise stress faster than scores.

Advice on how long to prepare varies widely, from a couple of months for students new to these tests to a year or more. No one has solid evidence for an exact figure. What everyone agrees on is starting early, building steady habits and avoiding last-minute cramming.

Practice or drilling?

The test providers' position is that these skills develop over a lifetime and that the best preparation is wide reading and critical thinking. Practice tests exist to help students get used to the style of the questions, not for intensive drilling. Others argue that preparing is sensible anyway, because unfamiliar timing and question styles hurt capable students. A fair middle path is steady practice with real attention to mistakes, not piles of drills.

A five-step loop for multiple-choice sections

  1. Do a practice set without the clock and aim for accuracy.
  2. For each wrong answer, work out why the right one is right.
  3. Write down a strategy for that question type and make it faster over time.
  4. Repeat until scores are consistently high.
  5. Then practise under timed conditions.

In ScholarPrep, that means Practice mode first, Targeted mode on weak topics, then Exam mode.

Written Expression

  • Spend a couple of minutes planning before you write.
  • Answer the prompt you are given. A memorised essay that does not fit is a risk.
  • Original ideas and clear organisation matter more than length, and more than perfect spelling.
  • Simple, accurate words beat impressive ones used wrongly.
  • Get a real person to read it and give feedback. That is the fastest way to improve.

Common mistakes

  • Starting too late and cramming in the final week.
  • Practising only the sections your child finds easy.
  • Never practising under time.
  • Doing piles of questions without reviewing why answers were wrong.
  • Adding pressure. Frame practice as building skills, and remember a scholarship is one pathway among several.

Quality over quantity

More practice is not always better. Rushing through huge piles teaches skimming. Fewer questions, reviewed properly, build more skill.
