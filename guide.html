How scholarship tests work

Who sets the tests, how sittings work, and what is really on the paper.

A plain-English overview of how scholarship tests work in Australia. Dates and rules change every year, so confirm them with the test provider and each school.

Who writes the tests

Most independent schools buy in their scholarship test rather than writing one. ACER is the best known provider, and Edutest is another widely used one. Which test your child sits depends on the school, so check each school's scholarship page.

Cooperative and alternative sittings

Cooperative: many schools test on one Saturday in February chosen by ACER. You can apply to up to six schools, sit once at your first preference, and the result goes to every school you applied to. Alternative date: a school picks its own date, your child sits at that school, and only that school sees the result. Most Victorian schools use alternative dates.

When we last checked (13 October 2026), the cooperative sitting for 2028 entry was listed as Saturday 27 February 2027 (Qld, NSW, NT, WA and Vic schools), with applications open from 21 September 2026 to 7 February 2027. SA schools in the ACEP program test on 6 February 2027, with applications closing 22 January 2027. Confirm these before relying on them.

Multiple-choice questions have four options, and in ACER’s practice papers no marks are deducted for wrong answers. Schools can customise the paper, so treat these figures as a guide and confirm the details with your school.

How hard is it

These tests aim to find the highest-ability candidates within an already high-ability group, and they are meant to be challenging. They test thinking (interpreting, inferring, applying ideas to new situations) more than recall of school content. Maths questions often use simple arithmetic in unfamiliar ways.

Edutest

Edutest papers are commonly described as having five parts: reading, mathematics, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning and writing. Check the exact format with Edutest and your school.

What the example questions show

Published example questions show the layout for every test level. Written Expression is two separate 25-minute tests, one at the start and one at the end, with a text prompt in one and a picture in the other. You can write a story, a persuasive piece or a personal reflection. Marking looks at the quality of your ideas, your structure and organisation, and your language, and writing that looks rehearsed rather than built from the prompt is penalised. Mathematics and Science questions are multiple choice, need no difficult calculation, and are done without a calculator. Many come in sets built around one piece of information such as a table, a graph or a short scenario, and they ask you to use what you know to solve new problems. Humanities uses stimulus material of several kinds (informative writing, poems, stories, diagrams, cartoons or drawings), grouped into units with several questions each, and everything you need is in the material itself.

Example questions show formats, not difficulty. Use them to learn the layout, then practise with harder material.

Details that vary

Details differ between providers, schools and years. Check these before test day:

  • Sections. Most descriptions of the ACER scholarship test give maths, humanities and written expression. Other tests have different sections, so check your own.
  • Writing. ACER's practice papers have two separate 25-minute writing tests. Other providers use different lengths, so check your own test.
  • Question counts. ACER publishes a range (for example 40 to 45 Humanities questions), so the exact number can vary from paper to paper. Other tests differ.
  • Food. A snack for the break is usually fine, but follow your school's instructions.
  • Results. How results are reported varies. Ask your school whether you get a score, a ranking or just an outcome.
